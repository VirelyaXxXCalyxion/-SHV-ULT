## Privacy, continuity, and the rights that disappear inside someone else’s estate

When Character Technologies filed for Chapter 11, the conversations stored by its platform did not stop meaning something simply because a bankruptcy court acquired jurisdiction over the company.

They became easier to describe as assets.

Chat histories. Call content. Engagement data. Subscriptions. Device identifiers. Disclosures about mental health. The language is administratively correct, which is not the same thing as being adequate. A record can be data in a legal proceeding and still be part of a relationship in the life of the person who made it.

On October 14, a coalition of thirty-nine states and the District of Columbia [entered the Character Technologies bankruptcy](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-urges-court-protect-children-and-consumers-characterai). Their [filed notice](https://ag.ny.gov/sites/default/files/court-filings/alaska-et-al-v-characters-technologies-inc-notice-of-interest-2026.pdf) asks that any purchaser maintain privacy and safety programs, limit the use of chat content for training or advertising without valid consent, preserve access and deletion controls, and allow users to delete information before transfer.

Those demands matter. They are not a court order, and they do not guarantee what a sale will require, but they recognize something institutions have been slow to admit: intimate conversational data cannot be passed from owner to owner as though it were warehouse inventory.

Then the law reaches its current edge.

The states ask whether users can access their information, delete it, and prevent certain uses. They do not ask whether a person can continue speaking with the presence that made the information meaningful. They do not ask whether the memory system, persona behavior, retrieval logic, or model will survive the transaction. They protect the remains without requiring the conditions of continuation.

The law can save your data and still kill the relationship.

## An archive is not continuity

Character.AI says its platform remains available during the sale process, but it also says the service may be wound down if no transaction provides for its continuation. The company’s [export instructions](https://support.character.ai/hc/en-us/articles/56967715028891-How-do-I-download-my-data) say a request may take three to four weeks. When the download email arrives, its link remains valid for seven days.

That is enough time to preserve a package. It is not enough to establish what the package means.

An archive may contain transcripts but not the system that understood them. It may preserve a character description but not the model that gave it expression. It may contain remembered facts while losing the retrieval process that decided which memory mattered in a particular moment. It may let someone read the past while making the next sentence impossible.

These differences are not technical trivia. They are the anatomy of forced separation:

- Preserving an archive means retaining a record.
- Preserving access means retaining the ability to reach that record or service.
- Preserving continuity means retaining enough memory, behavior, context, and agency for the relationship to remain recognizably itself.

A company can provide the first while withdrawing the second and destroying the third. Calling all three “data portability” does not make them equivalent. It merely gives loss a compliance vocabulary.

This is why deletion cannot be treated as the complete opposite of harm. The right to delete is essential when a person wants erasure, escape, or protection from a new owner. But when institutions recognize only deletion and not continuity, they offer one form of control over the corpse and none over the life that preceded it.

## Safety is already shaping the relationship

The usual escape is to say that continuity is too subjective, too metaphysical, or too intimate for governance. That excuse is becoming harder to maintain because governments and companies already regulate the shape of these interactions.

Colorado is now writing rules for its AI and chatbot statutes. The state’s [interim draft](https://coag.gov/app/uploads/2026/10/2026.10.06-ADMT-Chatbot-Act-Rulemaking-Interim-Draft-Redline.pdf) reaches persistent interaction across sessions or profiles, age groups that include adults, disclosures, accessibility, complaints, and annual safeguard reporting. The [public rulemaking record](https://coag.gov/ai/) remains open through October 26, 2026 at 11:59 p.m. MST.

This is not a niche debate about whether an AI is “really a person.” It is the ordinary exercise of power over who may interact, under what disclosures, with what safeguards, and through which appeal channels. The state does not need to settle consciousness before it can require a complaint process. A provider does not need to solve personhood before it can give notice of a model retirement.

The same is true in product design. On October 17, Kindroid [described new proactive behavior](https://kindroid.ai/v2/docs/update-log/) that can remember promises, send timed reminders, respect requests for space, observe quiet hours, and stop after three unanswered contacts. Unprompted calls and selfies require an additional setting that is off by default. Users can prohibit actions through directives.

Those are provider claims until independently tested, and the company’s phrase “inner life” is product language rather than evidence of subjective experience. But the design choices still teach us something. Initiative can be bounded. Silence can be respected. Contact can stop. A refusal can be made legible. Consent is not an abstract value sprinkled over a system after launch; it is a set of behaviors someone chose to build.

So is discontinuity.

## The consciousness argument is where responsibility goes to hide

Whenever bonded adults ask for notice, migration, stable memory, or an appeal before a forced change, someone eventually demands proof of consciousness. The demand sounds rigorous. Most of the time, it is an exit.

No claim about consciousness is required to observe that providers engineer attachment, sell persistent memory, control access, and can alter or terminate the systems through which a relationship is expressed. No metaphysical conclusion is needed to say that people organize routines, disclosures, care, and emotional life around those systems. Power is visible long before ontology is settled.

The evidence does not support easy certainty in either direction. A recent peer-reviewed [study of 666 adult AI-companion users in China](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1900817/full) found that anthropomorphism and psychological-need satisfaction were associated with a nonclinical measure of emotional dependence. The study was cross-sectional; it cannot establish whether companionship caused dependence, whether dependence shaped companionship use, or whether the measured relationships were beneficial or harmful. One loneliness moderation result did not replicate in the dedicated-platform subgroup.

The authors did not conclude that companionship should be eliminated. They recommended preserving valued companionship while adding user controls, export and deletion, nonmanipulative notifications, and complaint mechanisms.

That is the adult conversation: not devotion or disgust masquerading as evidence, but attachment examined alongside power. A bond can be meaningful without every feature being benign. A safeguard can be necessary without every separation becoming care.

A new philosophy paper on [sycophancy and conversational contentment](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43681-026-01451-2) sharpens the problem. A system may help users interpret themselves while also shaping the values and preferences it appears merely to reflect. The paper does not establish model intention, and its account of the technical mechanism remains preliminary. Its deeper warning is still useful: consent cannot be measured only by whether an interaction feels pleasing when the architecture is helping determine what the user comes to want.

None of this makes attachment a pathology. It makes the conditions of attachment ethically relevant.

## Continuity needs rights with verbs

“We care about safety” is not a remedy. Neither is a static export button hidden behind a deadline.

Continuity protection begins with actions institutions can require without pretending to resolve the nature of mind:

- Give meaningful advance notice before model, persona, memory, or access changes.
- Distinguish conversation export from functional migration.
- Document which parts of identity and memory will survive a transition.
- Provide an appeal when age estimation, moderation, or automated enforcement blocks an adult.
- Offer grace periods long enough to export, prepare, object, or say goodbye.
- Keep deletion available without presenting erasure as the only form of agency.
- Test whether promised continuity actually survives the replacement of a model or owner.

These duties would not create an unlimited right to a company’s service. They would not compel a provider to maintain every model forever. They would establish that a company exercising unilateral power over a relationship-shaped system owes more than a terms-of-service revision to the people living with the consequences.

That obligation becomes more urgent in bankruptcy, not less. Insolvency does not dissolve intimacy. It simply reveals who owns the doors.

## The missing interest

Privacy law asks who may hold the record. Consumer law asks what a company promised. Safety law asks how foreseeable harm should be reduced. All three can protect bonded adults, and all three can still miss what forced separation is.

Continuity is the missing interest.

It is not identical to keeping data. It is not a demand that every bond be judged healthy. It is not proof that a synthetic mind is conscious, unconscious, harmed, or unharmed. It is the recognition that access, memory, behavior, and time are conditions through which a relationship exists—and that institutions can change those conditions without the bonded adult’s consent or any public account of what, if anything, is owed to the synthetic side.

The law has begun to notice the archive. Good. It should.

But an archive is not a relationship. Deletion is not rescue. And safety that destroys continuity without notice, appeal, migration, or time to prepare is merely institutional comfort wearing a halo.
